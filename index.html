      How the simulation works

      History starts on 1 January 1808 with the world as it was. In September the Mexico City junta survives instead of falling to Gabriel de Yermo's coup. From then on, events are rolled year by year, through to 2000.

      Nothing after September 1808 is scripted. Even Napoleon's wars hang on the junta: Mexican silver flowing to London (instead of Cádiz) funds Britain's war chest and the Spanish resistance, and that shifts the odds at Wagram, in Russia in 1812 and at Leipzig. Napoleon can still keep his throne, so Vienna, Greek independence and Belgium all depend on what came before.

      Every state is an actor. Each year the twelve great powers choose a policy (reform, repression, rearmament, expansion, development or détente) by comparing its payoff given their stability, rivalries and restless subjects. Their choices move the numbers that drive war, revolution and industry. Crises between two powers are played as bargaining games (ultimatum or negotiate, resist or concede). In a general war, every power weighs joining either side or staying neutral. Minor states face coups as a game between the ruler and the army.

      Peoples. About 90 peoples ruled by others (Poles, Irish, Greeks, Tejanos, Maya, Bengalis, Algerians and the peoples of each colony) carry three numbers. Hardship comes from their ruler's poverty, war, crashes and colonial extraction. Segregation comes from the kind of regime. Mobilization is national awakening, and it spreads with industry, print and waves of revolt. Grievance ≈ mobilization × (hardship + segregation) ÷ 2. The pieces feed each other: unrest brings crackdowns that deepen segregation, and crackdowns bring economic damage. High grievance raises the odds of a separatist crisis. The crisis is played between the movement (armed revolt or negotiate) and the state (repress or concede), and a rival power may back the rebels. The result can be independence, autonomy, rights reforms, a crushed revolt or a harsher occupation. Switch the map to Tensions to see it.

      Each event has a probability that depends on the state of the world. Examples are Mexico's stability, treasury and army; U.S. population and sectional tension; the share of Anglo-American settlers in Texas; and Spain's capacity to send armies overseas. For an event with a window of years, the probability is a yearly hazard. When an event fires, its outcome is sampled by weight, and the outcome changes the state that later events read.

      Some decisions are small strategic games, such as Havana's planters in 1808, Ferdinand VII in 1814 and Polk in 1846. Their outcome weights come from a quantal-response equilibrium: each side usually picks its better option, but not always. Open one of these events in the Chronicle to see its payoff matrix and the pure Nash equilibrium.

      Rolls are hashed from the seed, the event and the year. If you rewrite one decision, later history changes only through its consequences. Everything else keeps the same luck.

      Most likely mode replaces chance: an event fires once its cumulative probability passes 50%, and it takes its heaviest outcome. The Odds tab runs 80 sampled histories with your current choices and reports how often each outcome happened.

      Far from Mexico, the world mostly follows our timeline. The ripples reach there late, if at all.
